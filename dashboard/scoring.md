# The Scoring Rubric

A framework for ranking roles 0–100. This is a *method*, not a prescription — the whole point is that it's calibrated to each user's own hunt. Nothing here is a default, and every number marked "example" is illustrative, not a recommendation.

## The one rule: a fixed budget

100 points, divided across the dimensions the user chooses. Fixed weights force honesty — dimensions compete, so nothing gets to be "also important." If everything matters, nothing does.

## Step 1 — the calibration interview

Before anything is scored, the agent interviews the user. These are the questions that actually matter:

1. **Money:** What's your floor — the walk-away number? What's your target? How should undisclosed comp be treated?
2. **Place:** Where will you live for this? What's commutable, what's a hard no, how do you feel about remote?
3. **The work itself:** What kind of work energizes you? What drains you? (Be honest — you'll interview better for roles you'd actually enjoy.)
4. **Employer:** What kinds of employers are you targeting — and why? Prestige, economics, trajectory, mission?
5. **Trajectory:** What does this job need to lead to?
6. **Dealbreakers:** What disqualifies a role instantly, no matter how well it scores elsewhere?
7. **Urgency:** Any deadlines or time pressure shaping the search?

## Step 2 — the proposed rubric

From the answers, the agent proposes:

- **Dimensions and weights** summing to 100. A typical starting menu: role relevance, compensation, employer tier, work type, location, recency, urgency — but the set belongs to the user. One dimension may be designated the *defining* dimension and deliberately overweighted (e.g. 30/100) when a single factor dominates the decision.
- **Tier definitions** for the employer dimension: 3–5 named tiers with point values and a one-line test each. ("Would I take a pay cut to work here?" sorts tiers fast.)
- **Anchors** per dimension: what a top score looks like and what a zero looks like, in concrete evidence terms.

The user approves or revises. **Nothing scores until they sign off.**

*Illustrative example (not a recommendation):*

| Dimension | Weight |
|---|---|
| Role relevance | 30 |
| Compensation | 25 |
| Employer tier | 20 |
| Location | 15 |
| Recency | 10 |

## The honesty mechanisms

These are the parts worth stealing regardless of the weights:

1. **Justification notes.** Every dimension score carries a one-line note defending it. If you can't defend the points in one line, you don't award them.
2. **Evidence over adjectives.** Score what the role *does*, not what the posting *says*. A buzzword in the intro paragraph is not substance; a responsibility spelled out in "what you'll do" is.
3. **Learned adjustments.** Every applied/dismissed verdict feeds back as a small nudge per dimension. Cap the total so it tunes the model without hijacking it. This is the model's memory of the user's *revealed* preferences — it will eventually disagree with their stated ones, and it'll usually be right.
4. **A named tiebreak.** When two roles land within a couple of points, a pre-agreed dimension decides (compensation is the classic).
5. **Hand calibration.** Score 10 roles by hand first. If everything clusters 70–80, the anchors are too generous — widen the spread until the top of the queue *feels* like the top of the queue. If the ranking doesn't change what the user would do Monday morning, recalibrate.

That's the rubric. The numbers belong to the user; the discipline belongs to the system.
